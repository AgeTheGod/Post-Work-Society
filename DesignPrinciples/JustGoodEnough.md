#   Just Good Enough

Solutions do not have to be perfect, they just have to be good enough to meet the needs of the people that are using them.

The "Just Good Enough" design principle dictates that a solution should only be developed to the point where it successfully solves the stated business problem and meets the business goals.
Instead of chasing flawless perfection or adding endless features, it prioritizes "_Function Over Perfection_" i.e. delivering a high-utility solution that satisfies the user's needs right now.

The key benefits are...

|                       |                                                                                                                                                                                |
|-----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Faster Time-to-Market | Speeds up development, allowing teams to launch products and gather real user feedback much quicker.                                                                           |
| Cost Efficiency       | Saves money by avoiding excessive hours spent tweaking visual pixels or writing code for edge-case scenarios that users may never encounter.                                   |
| Agility               | A "just good enough" design is much easier to iterate, pivot, or completely redesign because the team has not overly invested emotionally or financially in a rigid framework. |

"Just Good Enough" is the Antidote to Over-Engineering i.e. it prevents "feature creep" (adding unnecessary features) and "bike-shedding" (wasting time debating trivial design details) by answering the question "_does it do what it needs to do?_"

Having worked extensively in highly regulated environments and for UK Government departments "Gold-Plating" (a variation of "Feature Creep") is a particular risk because no matter what solution is bewing proposed there is always someone that says "Wouldn't it be nice if we could also..."
This Design Principle is meant to counteract that conversation.
