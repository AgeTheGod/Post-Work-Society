#   Evolution Not Revolution

The "Evolution, Not Revolution" principle states that lasting success and system stability are achieved through gradual, continuous, incremental improvements (evolution) rather than sudden, massive, and disruptive overhauls (revolution).

In product management, software engineering, and business change, this principle warns that tearing everything down to start from scratch is highly risky and usually unnecessary.
Instead, you should continuously adapt what already works, don't try to change everything at once, but instead evolve and improve over time.

As a minimum it means there must be a clear path from the current state to the desired future state, with clear milestones and checkpoints along the way to ensure that we are making progress towards our goals.

> [Note]
> "Revolution" is sometimes necessary but it should be the exception rather than the rule because revolution invariably involves a period of extreme turmoil before things settle down again.
> When it occurs "Revolution" is really a sign of completer systems failure and a lack of proper planning and foresight.

## Evolution vs. Revolution

People naturally resist sudden disruption and, from experience, if you introduce a completely way of doing things without a planned transition path then major disruption occurs. 
The table below highlights the key differences between evolutionary and revolutionary approaches.


| Feature        | Evolutionary Approach                     | Revolutionary Approach                                 |
|----------------|-------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Pace of Change | Slow, steady, and sequential.             | Fast, explosive, and sudden.                           |
| Risk Level     | Low; mistakes are small and easily fixed. | High; failure can break the entire business.           |
| Cost           | Spread out predictably over time.         | High upfront investment with uncertain ROI.            |
| User Reaction  | Smooth transition; low resistance.        | High friction; users get frustrated by sudden changes. |
