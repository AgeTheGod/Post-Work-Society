    prompt: how has the average life expectancy changed since 1900?

Global average life expectancy has more than doubled, climbing from roughly 32 years in 1900 to 73 years. This monumental shift represents one of humanity’s greatest modern achievements. [1, 2, 3, 4]
However, this statistical jump does not mean the typical adult in 1900 dropped dead at age 32. Instead, it reflects a profound change in the primary causes of death and the ages at which those deaths occur. [3, 5]
## 1. The Myth vs. Reality of "Short Lifespans"
In 1900, life expectancy data was heavily skewed by extremely high infant and child mortality rates. [5, 6]

*
* High-Risk Infancy: Statistically, if an individual survived past the age of 5, they often lived into their 60s or 70s.
* Shift to Older Ages: The modern increase in life expectancy means that death is being compressed into older ages rather than distributed heavily across childhood. For example, the probability of a UK newborn surviving to age 90 rose from a mere 3% (for males born in 1900) to over 54% for those born in 2020. [6, 7, 8, 9]
*

## 2. Regional Differences (Then vs. Now)
While the upward trajectory is universal, industrialized nations started from a higher baseline and have reached higher peaks than the global average: [3, 5, 10, 11, 12]

| Region / Country [1, 2, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18] | Life Expectancy in 1900 | Modern Life Expectancy |
|---|---|---|
| Global Average | ~31–32 years | ~73 years |
| United States | ~47 years | ~78.7 years |
| United Kingdom | ~45–48 years | ~81 years |

## 3. Key Historical Drivers of the Change
The transition occurred in two distinct, chronological waves: [3, 19, 20]
## First Half of the 20th Century: Beating Infectious Disease

*
* Sanitation and Water: The widespread deployment of filtered water, sewage systems, and better housing radically cut down waterborne illnesses like cholera and typhoid. [14, 16, 21, 22, 23]
* Early Medical Advances: The introduction of mass childhood immunisations and the discovery of penicillin/antibiotics stopped routine childhood infections from being fatal. [1, 19, 24]
* Nutrition: Safer supply chains and improved agricultural techniques solved chronic malnutrition in industrialized areas. [1, 14]
*

## Second Half of the 20th Century to Present: Managing Degenerative Diseases

*
* Cardiovascular & Cancer Care: As people survived infectious diseases, science shifted its focus to chronic conditions affecting older adults. Rapid drops in mortality from heart disease and stroke have allowed seniors to live much longer lives. [3, 25, 26]
* Lifestyle Changes: Large-scale public health interventions, most notably the decline in tobacco smoking, drastically improved cardiovascular and respiratory healthspan. [2, 3, 27, 28]
*

## 4. Modern Trajectories
Despite steady progress for over a century, the rate of increase has begun to experience notable shifts: [3, 17]

*
* Diminishing Gains: In highly developed countries like the UK and US, life expectancy improvements have slowed down or flattened since 2011.
* Global Disruptions: Temporary but sharp drops in life expectancy still occur during large-scale global crises, such as geopolitical shocks, the HIV/AIDS epidemic in sub-Saharan Africa, and the global COVID-19 pandemic. [2, 29, 30]
*

    prompt: What was the infant mortality rate as percentage of births?

Historically, infant mortality rates (defined as death within the first year of life) were staggeringly high, but they have plummeted significantly over the last century. [1, 2]
When looking strictly at infants under one year old, the global historical average was roughly 25% to 30% of all live births, which has now dropped to a global average of 2.4% in 2026. [1, 2, 3]
A direct comparison of infant mortality (under age 1) and child mortality (under age 5) as a percentage of live births shows the dramatic decline: [4]
## Global Mortality Rates as a Percentage of Live Births [5]

| Era / Year [1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8] | Infant Mortality Rate (Under Age 1) | Total Child Mortality Rate (Under Age 5) |
|---|---|---|
| Premodern / Historical | ~27% to 30% | ~46% to 50% (Nearly half of all children) |
| Year 1900 | ~15% to 20% globally (14% in developed countries) | ~41% globally |
| Year 1950 | ~16% globally | ~22.5% globally |
| Modern Day (2026) | ~2.4% globally | ~3.7% globally |

## Important Distinctions

* The "1 in 4" Rule: For most of human history up until the early 20th century, a family could expect that roughly 1 in 4 babies would not survive their first year of life. [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]
* The "1 in 2" Rule: When expanding the data to child mortality (all deaths before puberty/age 5), approximately half of all children died before reaching adulthood. [1, 6]
* The Industrial Divide (1900): By 1900, early sanitation was beginning to work. In modernizing countries like the UK and US, the infant mortality rate had already started dipping to about 14% (140 deaths per 1,000 births), while less industrialized areas remained well above 25%. [2]
* The Baseline Today: Today, in highly developed countries, infant mortality is a fraction of a percent, sitting between 0.3% and 0.5% (3 to 5 deaths per 1,000 live births). [2, 14, 15]

The steep collapse of these percentages is the single biggest statistical reason why the average human life expectancy figures jumped so high after 1900. [4]

## 1. Historical Breakdown (Circa 1900 Global Averages)
Because children are only at risk of dying in a specific year if they survive the previous one, demographers measure this using conditional probability (the chance of a child dying during that specific year of life). [5]

* Age 0 to 1 (Infancy): ~15% to 20% risk.
  Newborns faced acute vulnerability to birth complications, poor neonatal care, and immediate waterborne or airborne pathogens.
* Age 1 to 2 (Toddlerhood): ~4% to 5% risk.
  The risk dropped significantly but remained dangerous. This stage coincided with weaning off breastmilk, exposing toddlers to contaminated food and water, which led to fatal diarrhoeal diseases. [3, 4]
* Age 2 to 3: ~2.5% to 3.5% risk.
  As children grew more mobile, they encountered localized childhood diseases like scarlet fever, measles, and whooping cough.
* Age 3 to 4: ~2% to 2.5% risk.
  Immune systems began adapting, making common respiratory infections slightly less fatal than in infancy.
* Age 4 to 5: ~1.5% to 2% risk.
  This was the safest year of early childhood. Once a child passed their fifth birthday, their statistical probability of surviving into adulthood increased dramatically. [6]

When you add these individual yearly risks together across the 5-year window, they compound to the total historical under-5 mortality rate of ~41% around the year 1900.
------------------------------
## 2. Modern Breakdown (2026 Global Estimates)
Today, global child mortality has not only dropped massively to an overall under-5 rate of 3.7%, but the distribution across those five years has also shifted heavily toward the earliest days of life. [7, 8, 9]
According to data compiled by organizations like the [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/child-mortality-under-5-years) and [UNICEF](https://data.unicef.org/topic/child-survival/under-five-mortality/): [7, 10, 11]

* Age 0 to 1: ~2.4% risk.
  Nearly 80% of all under-5 deaths occur before a child turns one. Furthermore, almost half of those deaths happen within the first 28 days of life (the neonatal period), driven by premature births and birth trauma. [7, 8]
* Age 1 to 2: ~0.6% risk.
  Clean water and modern nutrition mean that weaning is no longer a high-risk transition.
* Age 2 to 3: ~0.3% risk.
  Mass vaccination campaigns have effectively neutralised historical killers like measles and diphtheria in most regions. [4]
* Age 3 to 4: ~0.2% risk.
* Age 4 to 5: ~0.2% risk.

## 3. The Takeaway
Both then and now, the first 365 days of life are the most critical survival hurdle. Historically, child deaths were spread heavily across all five years due to a lack of antibiotics and vaccines. In the modern era, medical advances have compressed childhood mortality almost entirely into the neonatal stage (the first month of life). [7, 8, 12, 13]

Around 1900, childhood mortality was heavily divided by social class, creating a stark survival gap between the rich and the poor. However, the nature of this inequality reveals a fascinating historical paradox: early on, wealth alone could not fully protect a child from the filthy urban environments of the Industrial Revolution. [1, 2, 3, 4]
------------------------------
## 1. The Statistical Divide (Circa 1900–1911)
When governments began officially tracking mortality by socio-economic class at the turn of the century, the data confirmed a steep social gradient. [2, 5]
According to data from the [UK historical census registers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11617806/) and comparative studies of the [1900 US Census](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/inequality-and-childhood-mortality-a-comparison-of-england-and-wales-1911-and-the-united-states-1900/4EFC870AD2389E7214B3BD3DFBD9ACEC): [2, 6]

*
* Unskilled/Working Class: Infant mortality sat between 13.3% and 15.2% (133 to 152 deaths per 1,000 live births).
* Middle and Upper Classes: Infant mortality was nearly cut in half, sitting at roughly 7.7% (77 deaths per 1,000 live births). [2]
*

This meant an infant born to a dockworker or miner was twice as likely to die before their first birthday than a baby born to a doctor, lawyer, or landowner. [1, 2]
## 2. The Micro-Environments of Class
The class divide was primarily driven by where people lived and what they could afford to eat:

[ Upper/Middle Class ] ---> Better nutrition, spacious housing, rural/suburban air, private doctors
[ Working/Poorer Class ] -> Crowded tenements, shared toilets, contaminated milk, polluted air


*
* Overcrowding & Disease: Poor families lived in densely packed urban tenements. If an airborne illness like measles or whooping cough entered a building, it spread to dozens of children immediately. [7]
* The "Summer Diarrhoea" Spike: Without refrigeration, milk spoiled rapidly in working-class slums. In the summer months, infants fed with cheap, contaminated cow's milk died in massive numbers from diarrhoeal dehydration. Wealthier families could afford iceboxes, fresh milk delivery, or wet nurses.
*

## 3. The Historical Paradox: Environment vs. Wealth
Surprisingly, demographers found that in the mid-to-late 1800s—just before 1900—the class gap was actually narrower than it was in 1911. [1, 5]
Historians Samuel Preston and Michael Haines noted that in the late 19th century, medical knowledge was so primitive that even wealthy parents did not understand germ theory. Rich and poor babies alike were treated with unsafe medical practices, and airborne epidemics swept through entire cities regardless of income. [3, 4]

*
* The Urban Penalty: A wealthy child living in a major city like London or New York often had a higher chance of dying than a poor child living in an isolated, clean rural village. [3, 4]
*

## 4. Why the Gap Widened Before It Shrank
As the year 1900 approached, the wealth gap temporarily widened. When science discovered germ theory and the importance of boiling milk, washing hands, and isolating sick patients, wealthy and educated families were the first to adopt these habits. [1, 3, 5, 8, 9]
It took several more decades for public infrastructure—such as clean city-wide water lines, public health clinics, and labor laws—to catch up and distribute those same survival advantages to the working class. [2, 10, 11]
## 5. The Modern Context
Today, the class gap in child mortality has shrunk to historic lows in developed nations, but it has not entirely vanished. Even with universal healthcare, infants born into lower socioeconomic demographics still face slightly higher statistical risks due to modern systemic issues like maternal stress, higher smoking rates during pregnancy, and unequal access to high-quality prenatal nutrition.
