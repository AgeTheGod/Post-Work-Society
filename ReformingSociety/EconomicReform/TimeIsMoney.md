# Using Time As Money

##  Why We Want To Do This

Given the rule that "_an hour of one persons time is worth exactly the same as an hour of another persons time_" the use of time rather than other arbitrary currency to measure the worth of something greatly simplifies both the economic system and the way that we measure wealth.

It also allows us to account for things that take time but do not necessarily have any intrinsic value at the time the cost in incurred. 
For example, Training and Education are traditionally seen as "loss leaders" that do not generate any income whilst the training is being undertaken but pay back a premium later on (assuming successful completion of the training).

Another benefit is that Time is a stable and universally agreed measure i.e. an hour is always an hour. 
Hence valuing something based on "_Time To Produce_" rather than the cost of resources makes it much easier to compare how value changes over time because we do not have to consider variables such as Price Inflation.

Finally, it is much more difficult to hoard time in the same way that we hoard money.

##   How It Works



##  How We Do It

This is not as big a change as it first appears to be because all accounting systems use numbers to represent money.  
The only difference is that we will be using time as the unit of account instead of some other currency.
The accounting system will still work the same way, it will just be using a different unit of account i.e. Elapsed Time Spent rather than Currency Spent.

With Multi-Currency Accounting Systems (and I've worked on a lot of these) it's even easier because, generally, it's simply a case of adding Time as a new entry into a Currency Code list extending the number of allowed values. 
The system will then be able to handle both Time and Currency as separate currencies, and even convert between them if required.  

In addition, given that nowadays nearly all Financial Transactions are Electronic Payments, we then just need to specify whether a particular payment is in Time or Money, and the system will handle it accordingly.

That gives us a transition path where during an interim period the cost of something can be priced in both Time and Currency and the buyer chooses which payment method they want to use.
Then we can gradually transition to using Time as Money for all transactions over time.

##  The Limit of Time Based Money




##  Spreading the Cost of Time

Amortization (and Depreciation) means spreading out a large cost or debt into smaller, equal parts over a set period of time. 
It has two main uses...
- Paying off a Loan.
  * The scheduled repayment of a loan (such as a mortgage) through regular installments.
  * Principal and interest: Each payment covers part of the borrowed money (principal) and the cost of borrowing (interest).
- Spreading the cost of large up-front capital purchases e.g. Tools, Equipment and Real Estate costs
  *  Cost is spread over the "_expected useful economic life_" of the Capital Goods being purchases
- Spreading the cost of non-physical business assets such as software, patents, copyrights, or trademarks. 
  * Instead of recording the whole cost at once, a small part of the cost is written off each year of the asset's useful life.
  * Similar to depreciation: Depreciation does the exact same thing, but for physical items like trucks or office chairs.

There are many different amortization methods, based strictly on the type of asset being amortized, used for spreading the cost but in all cases the aim being to reduce the outstanding balance to zero by the agreed amortization end-date.

For financial accounting purposes, non-physical capital costs (intangible assets) are amortized over their estimated useful life, which generally ranges from 3 to 20 years. 

For tax purposes, laws strictly mandate the exact timelines allowed.

In general corporate bookkeeping, companies match the amortization period to how long the asset will generate money. [6, 7]
* Software & Capitalised Tech: 3 to 5 years. Rapid tech obsolescence means software rarely carries value beyond this point. [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]
* Patents & Copyrights: 10 to 20 years. This is usually capped by the statutory lifespan of the legal protection, or the length of a specific contract. [2, 9, 13, 14, 15]
* Goodwill & Acquired Brands: 5 to 10 years (unless local rules like US GAAP require "impairment testing" instead of regular amortization). Under rules like UK FRS 102, if a business cannot reliably guess the lifespan, it defaults strictly to 10 years. [4, 16, 17]
* Franchises & Licenses: The exact term of the contract. If you buy a 7-year commercial operating license, it is amortized over precisely 7 years. [9, 18, 19]


## Standard Timelines for Tax Deductions

Tax authorities rarely allow businesses to "guess" asset lifespans. 
Instead, they write specific periods into tax codes to standardise deductions. [4]

| Asset Type | United States (IRS) | United Kingdom (HMRC) | Canada (CRA) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goodwill, Trademarks & Customer Lists | 15 Years (Fixed statutory period under Section 197) | Fixed 4% or 7.14% per year (if qualifying for specific tax regimes) | 5% per year on a declining balance (Class 14.1) |
| Off-the-Shelf Software | 3 Years (36 months) | Expensed immediately or over 3–5 years | Fully expensed or 5%–100% depending on the specific asset class pool |
| Business Startup Costs | 15 Years (For costs exceeding the initial $5,000 threshold) | Evaluated under standard corporation tax deduction rules | Tracked and deducted via the standard asset pools |
| Research & Development Costs | 5 Years (Domestic) / 15 Years (Foreign) | Expensed or handled under specialized R&D tax credits | Deducted through specialized SR&ED tax credit pools |

## Key Blind Spot: Loan Amortization vs. Capital Costs

If you are looking at "capital costs" because you took out a commercial loan to buy a physical asset (like heavy machinery or real estate), lenders use a different logic. Lenders tie the loan amortization period directly to the physical life of the item being financed: [20]

* Commercial Real Estate: 20 to 25 years.
* Heavy Equipment / Machinery: 10 to 12 years.
* Company Vehicles: 3 to 5 years. [20]


The main difference is the type of asset being written off. Both methods spread the cost of an asset over its useful life, but they apply to entirely different asset categories. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

## Core Differences

| Feature       | Amortization                  | Depreciation                   |
|---------------|-------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Asset Type    | Non-physical (Intangible)     | Physical (Tangible)            |
| Examples      | Patents, software, trademarks | Buildings, vehicles, machinery |
| Salvage Value | Almost always zero            | Usually has a residual value   |
| Calculation   | Usually Straight-Line only    | Multiple methods allowed       |

## Detailed Breakdown

* Physical vs. Non-Physical: Depreciation handles things you can physically touch, like a delivery truck or a factory machine. Amortization handles legal rights or intellectual property, like a software licence or a copyright. [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]
* Salvage Value: Physical assets usually retain some worth at the end of their life (e.g., selling an old truck for scrap metal). Intangible assets expire and are typically worth absolutely nothing at the end of their term. [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]
* Calculation Methods: Amortization is almost always calculated using the straight-line method, meaning the exact same amount is written off every year. Depreciation often uses accelerated methods (like reducing balance) because physical items lose value much faster in their first few years of use. [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]
