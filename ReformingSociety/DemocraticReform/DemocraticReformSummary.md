##  The Problems with Current UK Democracy

In the UK, we have a parliamentary democracy, which means that we elect representatives to make decisions on our behalf.
This is a good system in theory, but in practice it has a number of problems.

The first problem is that our representatives are often more interested in serving the interests of their political party and their donors than in serving the interests of the people.

Similar issues arise in other democracies around the world.

###  Fragmented Politics

One of the strong points of UK Democracy is the multi-party system which allows for a wider range of views to be represented in Parliament and avoids the extremer polarization that we generally see in two-party democracies (e.g. the USA).
In the UK elections are no longer a "Red or Blue Team" decision, but rather a "Red, Blue, Green, Yellow, Purple, Orange, etc. Team" decision. 
Nationally we have a 5-party choice with additional choices in some regional and local levels (Scottish Nationalists, Plaid Cymru, Ulster Unionists, Hackney First).

This allows for a more diverse range of views to be represented in Parliament and it also allows for more coalition governments to be formed, which can lead to more compromise and more effective governance.

This I think is a welcome side effect of the "Information Age" where, via Social Media, it is...
- much easier for people to access information and to connect with others who share their views
- alternative viewpoints, such as what I'm writing here, can be easily published and shared with a wider audience
- new political parties and movements that challenge the status quo can quickly form and gain support, which can lead to a more dynamic and responsive political system

Unfortunately, this fragmented political landscape also has it's downsides such as...
- It can make it more difficult to form stable governments and to implement policies that have broad support.
- can lead to a situation where a party can win a majority of seats in Parliament without winning a majority of the vote, which can lead to a lack of representation for a significant portion of the population.

This is particularly the case at Local Government level where there are often many different parties and independent candidates running for office.

###  "First Past The Post" (FPTP)

A consequence of Fragmented Politics is that the "First Past the Post" voting systems means an election can be won with a much smaller percentage of the vote (i.e. for N parties the percentage of the vote needed to win can be as low as 100% / N) so 5 choices only requires the wiiner to get 20% of the vote leaving 80% unrepresented.


### Archaic Voting System

However, the method of running elections is pretty archaic and it is not really fit for purpose in the modern world. 
We have...
- Manual voting cards mailed to each registered elector
- Manual counting of votes at polling stations
- A first-past-the-post system that often results in a majority government being elected with less than 50% of the vote, which means that a significant portion of the population is not represented
- A lack of proportional representation, which means that smaller parties are often underrepresented in Parliament
- An "All or Nothing" approach to voting, which means that voters have to choose between a limited number of options and they can't express their preferences in a more nuanced way

##  Roadmap to Democracy

- We have the Internet (and Virtual Private Networks) that allow us to securely and reliably vote online from anywhere in the world.
- Pretty much everyone of voting age has multiple electronic devices that they can use to access the Internet and to vote online.
- We have the technology to do this securely and reliably, and we have the infrastructure to support it.
- We have the experience of other countries that have implemented online voting, such as Estonia, which has been doing it successfully for over a decade.

This isn't a "Can we do it?" question, it's a "When can we do it?" question.
