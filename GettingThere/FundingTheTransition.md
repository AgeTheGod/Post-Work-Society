#   Funding The Transition

Setting up a Post Work Society would cost a lot of Money to transfer critical industries into public ownership i.e. buy the outstandibng shares on the open market to convert those companies into state owned enterprises.

80 years ago the Socialist solution would be easy - simply "nationalize" the industries that we need to take control over.
However, it's not as simple as that nowadays because most of the key industries are actually now owned by external parties and to simply confiscate them would cause all kinds of problems with many other countries.

Basically, we should not understimate the political power that existing Wealth Management Corporations such as Blackrock and State Street have and their abolity to exercise that political power to retaliate against any attempt "steal their property" from them.

Even though it's a challenge to convert existing companies we can still make decisions about how we want the market to function and define constraints on future suppliers into that market.

##  Electricity Generation

When assessing the UK’s aggregated electricity producers, the financial dynamic shifts away from National Grid’s steady, regulated wire-and-pipe monopoly. Instead, the major power generators (such as SSE plc, Centrica plc, and Drax Group) are exposed directly to wholesale energy market volatility, raw commodity prices, and the heavy costs of building wind farms and biomass facilities. [1, 2, 3]
Because the landscape is fragmented and includes foreign state-owned giants (like France's EDF or Spain's Iberdrola/ScottishPower) whose UK-only segments aren't standalone public stocks, an exact "aggregated index" isn't cleanly listed.

However, looking at the combined financial footprint of the major UK-listed electricity generation sector (using SSE, Centrica, and Drax as the core proxy sector), the aggregated summary over the last 5 years outlines a distinct narrative:

#### Key Observations on Market Coverage

* The 2023 Revenue Spike: Aggregate revenues rocketed to £67.50 billion in 2023. This was heavily driven by the tail-end of the global energy crisis, where wholesale gas and electricity prices spiked to record-breaking highs. [7, 8]
* The Margin Compression: While National Grid enjoys expanding profit margins (~18.3% in 2026), power producers run on significantly leaner net margins (averaging 4% to 7%).
* Generating companies bear the brunt of windfall taxes (such as the UK's Electricity Generator Levy) and extreme price fluctuations in natural gas and carbon credits. [9]
* Decoupled Market Cap vs NAV: The net assets (NAV) of the producers did not contract when stock prices fluctuated. This is because companies like [SSE plc](https://www.sse.com/) have been aggressively ploughing capital back into the ground—investing upwards of £3 billion to £3.6 billion annually into offshore wind networks and hydro-storage. [1, 10]
* Market Share Consolidation: The group's total percentage of market coverage expanded slightly from 22.4% to 25.5% over the five years. This steady increase is driven primarily by SSE launching major offshore wind assets and Drax expanding its flexible renewable generation footprint. [3, 7]
* Revenue vs Volume Disconnect: Look closely at 2023: the group's market generation coverage hit its lowest point at 22.4%, yet its total revenue skyrocketed to an all-time high of £67.50 billion. This explicitly highlights that the 2023 financial windfall was purely a result of sky-high wholesale price spikes following the energy crisis, rather than the companies producing or supplying more physical electricity.
* The Foreign/Private Shadow Market: The remaining ~75% of the UK electricity production landscape is heavily fragmented. Because vast portions of the UK wind and nuclear fleets are owned by multi-national corporations like [EDF Energy Holdings](https://www.edfenergy.com/) (France) or [RWE](https://www.rwe.com/en/) (Germany), a major chunk of the UK's industrial power value flows through entities not listed directly on the London Stock Exchange. [2]
