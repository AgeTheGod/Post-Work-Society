#   Modern Monetary Theory

Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) is an macroeconomic framework asserting that countries with complete control over their own currencies cannot run out of money. 

It's based on the fact that all modern currencies are Fiat Money (sse [What Is Money](./WhatIsMoney) and, unlike a household, a Currency-issuing Nation does not need to collect taxes before it can spend. 

The core assumptions (some would say "principles" but they really aren't) of Modern Monetary Theory are...

|                            |                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
|----------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Currency Issuance          | Governments print their own sovereign money.                                                                                                                                                      |
| No Default Risk            | Issuing nations cannot go bankrupt because they always have ownership of their national resources.                                                                                                |
| Spending Precedes Taxation | The government must spend money into existence before citizens can use it to pay taxes. Taxes serve to drive demand for the currency and regulate economic activity.                              |
| Inflation Control          | Taxes reduce the private money supply.                                                                                                                                                            |
| Natural Resource Limits    | Real resources limit spending, not money. If spending exceeds the productive capacity of the economy ((see [Factors Of Production](./FactorsOfProduction)) then prices rise and inflation occurs. | 
|                            | A government deficit is simply a surplus for the private sector. When the state spends more than it taxes, it deposits net wealth into the economy.                                               | 

Given these assumptions Modern Monetary Theory asserts that a Currency-issuing Nation can simply create new money to fund public expenditure as the need arises.

## Key Criticisms and Risks

* Hyperinflation Risks: Excessive money printing can trigger rapid inflation.
* Political Pressure: Politicians may overspend without raising stabilizing taxes.
* Currency Devaluation: Massive supply increases can weaken international exchange rates.
* Central Bank Independence: Merging fiscal and monetary policy risks political interference.

##  Who Uses Modern Monetary Theory

No country explicitly states that it officially practices Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) as a formal policy framework (same as no Country is officially "_Socialist_" or "_Capitalist_"). 

However, economists view Modern Monetary Theory as an operational description of how modern fiat currencies already function. 
From this perspective, any nation with full monetary sovereignty implicitly operates under Modern Monetary Theory mechanics.

Japan is the most frequently cited example of MMT principles in action (though its government explicitly denies practicing it) given 
- the massive Debt, No Default: Japan's public debt-to-GDP ratio has soared past 260%. Standard economic theory predicted bankruptcy or hyperinflation, but neither occurred.
- Central Bank Backing: The Bank of Japan routinely buys massive amounts of government bonds to sustain low interest rates, essentially monetising the debt.
- However Japan does not follow all Modern Monetary Theory policy recommendations as it frequently implements austerity and consumption tax hikes.

In addition during the recent 2008 Financial Crisis and COVID-19 pandemic quite a few other nations moved towards MMT-style behaviors such as...
- In the United States the US Federal Reserve printed trillions of dollars to directly purchase government debt, funding massive stimulus checks and relief programs without raising taxes beforehand.
- In the United Kingdom the Bank of England temporarily extended direct overdraft facilities to the government, showcasing that the central bank can directly fund state spending when required.
- In Canada and Australia both central banks bought unprecedented shares of domestic government bonds to guarantee state spending room, operating under the MMT premise that money supply adapts to federal funding needs.

### A Caveat 

According to the pure Modern Monetary Theory many countries are fundamentally blocked from using this approach because they do not monetary sovereignty.
This theoretically includes all members of the European Union (e.g. Germany and France) and the United States of America (e.g. California and Texas) where the Euro and Dollar are controlled by a separate Central Bank.

However, in practice all of these Eurozone Countries and American States continue to actively issue their own individual Government Bonds in addition to separate "Federal Bonds" issued by the respective Central Bank. 

This reflects the fact that most Geo-Political Regions generally exist in a hierarchy (see [What Is Society](./WhatISSociety)) where regional governments can "inherit" or be granted the ability to raise their own finances simply by being a member of the currency-issuing body.

##  Comparison to Traditional Keynesian Economics

The fundamental difference lies in how spending is financed and what limits the government. 

Traditional Keynesian economics views government spending as constrained by revenue and borrowing capacity. 
Modern Monetary Theory views government spending as constrained solely by real economic resources and inflation.

| Feature          | Traditional Keynesianism          | Modern Monetary Theory               |
|------------------|-----------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Spending Source  | Taxes and borrowing (bonds).      | Direct money creation.               |
| Role of Taxes    | Funds government expenditures.    | Regulates inflation and demand.      |
| Deficit View     | Necessary during recessions only. | Normal; represents private savings.  |
| Unemployment Fix | Interest rate cuts and stimulus.  | Permanent Federal Job Guarantee.     |
| Primary Risk     | Crowding out private investment.  | Hyperinflation from resource limits. |

Despite these friction points, both schools belong to the post-Keynesian lineage in that they both reject classical supply-side theories, agree that free markets fail to create automatic full employment, and view active government spending as the primary engine for economic growth.

##  Inflation

The surge of global inflation over recent years—sparked by the COVID-19 pandemic, supply chain shocks, and geopolitical crises—has intensified the debate over Modern Monetary Theory (MMT), shifting it from a theoretical academic discussion to a real-world policy battleground. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
Mainstream economists argue that recent price spikes prove MMT is dangerously flawed, while MMT advocates claim the crisis actually validates their framework.

Critics of MMT, including traditional Keynesian Economists and Monetarists, argue that recent history serves as a direct warning against MMT principles.
- The Stimulus Blame: Opponents argue that massive fiscal spending in countries like the US and UK during the early 2020s directly caused the high cost-of-living crisis. They view this as empirical proof that printing money to fund deficits triggers hyperinflation.
- The Political Impossibility: MMT states that when inflation rises, the government must raise taxes to drain money out of the economy. Critics note that during a severe cost-of-living crisis, no politician would realistically vote to raise taxes on struggling citizens, exposing a fatal flaw in MMT practical governance model.
- Failure of the Core Premise: Traditionalists point out that central banks had to aggressively raise interest rates to finally bring inflation back down toward targets, a tool MMT dismisses as blunt and unnecessary.

On the other hand, MMT advocates argue that recent inflation has actually played out exactly as their model predicted.
- The Real Resource Constraint: MMT has always maintained that the true limit to spending is not money, but real resources. Advocates argue that inflation was not caused by over-printing money, but by physical, supply-side bottlenecks: semiconductor shortages, energy shocks, and labor constraints.
- Targeted Tools vs. Blunt Interest Rates: MMT defenders argue that traditional interest rate hikes did little to fix the structural roots of recent inflation (like food and energy prices) and instead penalized everyday borrowers. They argue the recent economic climate proves the need for MMT preferred inflation fixes: targeted corporate windfall taxes, strategic price regulations, and investing to relieve supply-chain bottlenecks.
- The Terms of Debate Have Shifted: MMT economists point out that mainstream discussions have largely abandoned "deficit hawkishness." Governments are no longer asking "_How will we pay for it?_" but rather "_Will this spending cause inflation?_" which is the fundamental question MMT says they should be asking.

Either way, the debate has permanently altered the macroeconomic landscape and mainstream economic policy has increasingly acknowledged that supply-side capacities and physical limits (like green energy and AI infrastructure constraints) dictate economic success far more than arbitrary debt numbers.

However, MMT has lost considerable political momentum as a prescriptive policy tool, as the public and policymakers remain highly risk-averse to any theories associated with cash expansion.

##  Some Caveats

Having said all that I'll opening admit that I'm definitely a Modern Monetary Theory advocate but with some additional caveats to restrict what can be done...
- Governments should only create new money to fund the creation of other national assets i.e. building long-term infrastructure
- Ideally Government Debt should be sold into the traditional Bond Markets to be funded by Private Investment (which would then reverse it out from the government funded National Monetary Ledger).
- Government Debt that is not sold into the Bond Market needs to be income generating so that there is a measurable "**Return on Investment**". "_What's the benefit?_" should always be a question with a known quantifiable answer before investment takes place 
- The repayment term of the Debt should be less than the life expectancy of the asset being funded i.e. no point in taking 10 years to repay the finance on an asset with a 5-year life expectancy. Any asset needs to at least pay for itself whilst still operational. 

Finally, using debt to fund day-to-day operational costs is a recipe for financial disaster - it always has been and always will be. 

## The Job Guarantee

A secondary aspect of Modern Monetary Theory advocates for a centrally funded, locally administered job guarantee which serves as a macro-economic stabilizer creating a baseline for wages and achieving true full employment.
As a principle this one actually goes back to the 1950 / 1960's in the UK where the government, through the ownership of nationalized industries and local government services, was the employer of last resort for many of the working population.
