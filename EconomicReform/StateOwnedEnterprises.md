#   How To Deliver Public Services to Society

##  How We Got To Where We Are

### Nationalized Industries

It's no secret in the UK that Nationalized Industries directly owned by the UK Government were a complete failure during the 20th century for many reasons.

- They were a drain on the public finances and that 
- They were frequently the target of Political interference with unclear objectives being set by changing governments and that led to a lack of accountability and a lack of focus on delivering value to the public.
- They were a source of inefficiency - most of the nationalized industries employed many more people than they needed as a means to hid the true national unemployment numbers.  

All of which resulting in a very poor service to the public and a very poor return on the investment of the public's money and obviously wew do not qwant to go back to that method of providing public services to Society.

### Privatization

When Margaret Thatcher’s administration took office in 1979, the Morrisonian model was heavily criticized for creating inefficient monopolies that drained public funds. 
It consequently became government policy throughout the 1980s to dismantle this structure, the government used a specific organizational playbook:

1. Financial Tightening: The Treasury aggressively shrank EFLs, forcing industries to cut staff and close unprofitable facilities (such as coal pits and steel mills) to prepare them for commercial survival.
2. Corporate Restructuring: Statutory corporations were stripped of their public legal status and transformed into public limited companies (PLCs) under normal company law.
3. Flotation and Deregulation: The state’s shares in these newly formed PLCs were sold on the London Stock Exchange to institutional and retail investors, replacing ministerial control with independent sector regulators (such as Oftel for British Telecom in 1984 and Ofgas for British Gas in 1986).

### Private Equity Corporations

However, delivering public services through private enterprises has proved equally bad in many cases, because private enterprises are primarily motivated by profit and that can lead to a focus on short-term gains rather than long-term sustainability and that can lead to a focus on cutting costs rather than investing in quality and that can lead to a focus on maximizing shareholder value rather than delivering value to the public.


##  State Owned Enterprises

So, given that the Morrisonian model of Nationalized Industries and the Thatcherite model of Privatization have both proved to be very bad ways to deliver public services to Society, what are the alternatives?

Top of my list would be State Owned Enterprises, which are businesses that are partially or fully owned by the government but operate as individual Limited Liability Corporations.

As Allissa Cole writes in the preamble to ["why countries need a legal framework to govern state ownership of companies"](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/2023/09/22/why-countries-need-a-legal-framework-to-govern-state-ownership-of-companies/)...

"_**Governments often acquire ownership stakes or restrict foreign investments in strategic companies. 
But few countries have an ownership policy or equivalent. 
Such lack of clarity may lead to boards being either ineffective or bypassed altogether. 
... governments with important SOE portfolios must develop a legal framework governing companies that receive state investment.**_"

Unfortunately the UK does not have any specific legislation related to State Owned Enterprises and that is a problem because it means that there is no clear legal framework governing the operation of State Owned Enterprises and that can lead to a lack of accountability and a lack of focus on delivering value to the public.

France, on the other hand, does have a specific legal framework for governing State Owned Enterprises as part of its "**Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**" framework defining investment restrictions on designated "Strategic Sectors" that are critical to national security and economic sovereignty. 

The regulatory framework and specific mechanisms France uses to restrict and monitor investment in its SOEs and strategic industries are structured across several layers:
1. Tightened FDI Screening Thresholds : The Ministry of the Economy screens foreign investments based on the origin of the capital and the level of corporate control being targeted:
   * The 10% Voting Rights Rule: For publicly listed French companies—including partially privatized SOEs listed on regulated markets—any non-European investor crossing a 10% threshold of voting rights triggers a mandatory state review. Initially a temporary measure, this rule was made permanent by Decree No. 2023-1293.
   * The 25% Unlisted Corporate Rule: For unlisted French entities, any non-EU/EEA investor attempting to acquire more than 25% of voting rights must file for prior state authorization.
   * Acquisition of Control: Regardless of nationality (including EU/EEA-based investors), any transaction that results in a foreign entity gaining outright corporate control or buying an active branch of a strategic business requires explicit ministerial clearance.
2. Application and Compliance Safeguards
   * Mandatory Disclosures: To bypass the use of proxy shell corporations, French FDI rules require applicants to completely unmask their corporate structure. Investors must declare any substantial capital ties or financial support they have received from any foreign state or non-EU public body over the previous five years.
   * Mitigation Measures: Outright rejections are rare, but French authorities actively impose heavy mitigation constraints. About half of all approved strategic transactions are bound by strict conditions, such as keeping R&D facilities on French soil, safeguarding domestic employment, or preserving the French military's supply lines.
   * Penalties: Undertaking a transaction without prior ministerial approval can result in the deal being legally invalidated, along with heavy criminal or administrative fines reaching up to €50,000 per day.
3. Defined "Strategic Sectors" that are subject to enhanced scrutiny:
   * Energy and Utilities: Low-carbon energy, energy storage, and critical infrastructure.
   * Technology and Defense: Cybersecurity, artificial intelligence, quantum computing, photonics, and aerospace.
   * Public Services and Security: Food safety, public health, print/digital media, and prison security.
   * Critical Raw Materials: Supply chains directly linked to industrial manufacturing.

In addition to these specific legal mechanisms, France also maintains a broader policy framework that emphasizes economic sovereignty and strategic autonomy. 

This includes...
* State Vetoes so that even if an SOE is publicly traded, the French state often retains structural legal blocks to prevent hostile or unwanted foreign takeovers:
* Designates "**Domestic Champions**" that are critical to the national interest even where the company has no other direct state investment. 
* Uses "**Golden Shares**" (actions spécifiques) giving the government a permanent right to veto the sale of critical assets, block specific investors from buying large blocks of shares, or deny changes in corporate strategy.
* Direct Intervention using national security mandates to aggressively intercept deals. The Ministry of the Economy regularly blocks or heavily modifies proposed foreign acquisitions—such as blocking private equity sales of aerospace components or forcing extensive operational conditions on buyers.

Bottom line with these are that infrastructure generally requires a lot of up-front money to build and then it generates a steady stream of revenue over a long period of time once it's up and running.

##  Government Issued Debt Investment

Where there is significant on-going capital investment to build or modernize long-term infrastructure then Equity Investment is rarely seen as the best option to raise the money required.

In most cases the better option is to raise the money through Debt Investment i.e. borrow the money through a secured loan whicvh is then paid back over time from revenue generated by the borrower. 
This is classic capital investment that pretty much all Limited Liability Corporations already engage in so not really very radical.

The radical bit that a number of European countries have done successfully is that the government designates itself as the lender of first resort for certain types of investment and then issues government bonds to raise the necessary capital to fund those investments.
The benefits are that...
- Governments can typically borrow money at much lower interest rates than private companies, and that can make it more cost-effective to fund public services.
- The debt is secured by the borrowers assets (same principle as per private lenders) 
- It's the Borrower, not the Government, that actually pays opf the Debt, so does not become part of the National Borrowing and does not add to the National Debt.

##  Golden Shares

The French Foreign Direct Investment framework includes the use of "golden shares" (actions spécifiques) in partially privatized giants. These legal vehicles give the government a permanent right to veto the sale of critical assets, block specific investors from buying large blocks of shares, or deny changes in corporate strategy.

We also have these in the UK and the UK Government has used them in the past to protect certain companies, such as British Aerospace and Rolls Royce Holdings, from hostile takeovers.

In fact, we used to have a lot of these because every privatization through through the 1980's originally came with a "Golden Share" restriction but they've slowly been cancelled over the years and the privatised companies changed hands and various Golden Shares have been legally challenged by some companies and investors.

Note: France doesn't have this "legal challenge" problem because the have a FDI framework that deals with it. The problem in the UK is because we don;t have that framework to rely on.
